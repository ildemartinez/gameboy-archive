title: Model Codes & Identification
description: Model numbers for every Game Boy system and related hardware, how to read them, and how to judge originality and authenticity.

Every Game Boy system carries a Nintendo model number, usually printed on the rear label. The model number is the most reliable quick way to identify which system you have. Shell color and branding are not reliable, because shells are often replaced. This page lists the model numbers and explains how collectors and technicians build an evidence-based identification.

## Building an identification

Identification is strongest when several independent pieces of evidence agree. In rough order of reliability:

1. **Internal board markings** — the silkscreened board identifier, chip markings, and date codes. These are the hardest to change. For the DMG-01, see [PCB Revisions](/consoles/dmg-01/pcbs-revisions/overview/).
2. **Rear label** — model number, regional and regulatory text, and serial number. Labels can be reproduced or moved between shells, so check that the label matches the rest of the console.
3. **Shell and molded details** — color, finish, screw type, and molded text. Aftermarket shells are common.
4. **Packaging and paperwork** — useful provenance, but boxes and manuals are often reunited with consoles they did not ship with. See [Boxes & Packaging](/consoles/dmg-01/documentation-merchandise/packaging/) and [Manuals](/consoles/dmg-01/documentation-merchandise/manuals/).

Record each observation separately and state what was not checked. [Documenting a Game Boy Console](/consoles/example/) provides a recording checklist.

## Serial numbers

Serial numbers identify an individual unit, but the archive does not yet have a verified scheme for decoding them into production dates, factories, or board revisions. Record the serial exactly as printed. Do not use it alone to infer what is inside the console.

## Originality and authenticity

Collectors usually need to answer three different questions:

- **Is it genuine Nintendo hardware?** Check board markings and chip labels against photographed reference units such as those in the [Game Boy hardware database](https://gbhwdb.gekkio.fi/).
- **Is it in factory configuration?** Look for replaced shells, buttons, lenses, screens, or labels. Many consoles combine original boards with aftermarket parts. The DMG-01 [Modifications](/consoles/dmg-01/modifications/) page describes common changes.
- **Is it the edition it claims to be?** Special and promotional editions are hard to verify. The [Limited Editions](/consoles/dmg-01/physical-design/shell-housing/limited-editions/) page explains why provenance matters more than appearance.

### Cartridges

Reproduction cartridges — copies built on non-original boards — are common. The external label and shell are the easiest parts to imitate. The board identifier, ROM and controller chip markings, and the cartridge header data provide stronger evidence. See [Game Boy Cartridges: identification and care](/cartridges/game-boy-cartridges/#identification-and-care).

A detailed guide to telling specific counterfeit consoles and cartridges apart from originals is planned. It will be added only with sourced comparison photographs.

## Preservation

- Remove batteries before long-term storage; leaking cells corrode contacts. See [Battery Compartment](/consoles/battery/).
- Battery-backed cartridge saves are lost when the cartridge battery runs out. Back up important saves with suitable tools before the battery fails. See [Game Boy Cartridges: saving progress](/cartridges/game-boy-cartridges/#saving-progress).
- Keep packaging, manuals, and inserts together with a note of what is known to be original to the set.
